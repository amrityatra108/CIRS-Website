The man

The Man
Behind the
Vision.

He was born Balakrishna Menon in Ernakulam on 8 May 1916, took a degree in English literature, and came to public life as a journalist and a participant in the freedom movement — work that brought him imprisonment and, by 1942, grave illness.

What followed was not a career but a reversal. He went to Rishikesh in September 1947 intending, by his own account, to expose the swamis as frauds; he met Swami Sivananda instead, and stayed. On Mahashivaratri, 25 February 1949, he received sannyasa and the name Swami Chinmayananda Saraswati. He then went up to Uttarkashi to study Vedanta under Swami Tapovan Maharaj.

He might have remained there. The decision that shaped everything after it — including this school — was to come back down and teach Vedanta in English, in public, to people who had never been told it was theirs to study.

  2. 1940s

    The Freedom Movement

    A degree in English literature, then journalism and the movement for independence. He was imprisoned, and by 1942 gravely ill. He had seen enough of the country to be asking what would hold it together once it was free.

  3. 1947

    Rishikesh

    In September he reached the Divine Life Society and met Swami Sivananda. The journalist stayed on as a student.

  4. 1949

    Sannyasa

    On Mahashivaratri, 25 February, Swami Sivananda initiated him into sannyasa as Swami Chinmayananda Saraswati.

  5. 1949

    Uttarkashi

    He went up to the Himalaya to study under Swami Tapovan Maharaj, one of the most exacting Vedantins of the age, and remained his student.

    {{FIG:uttarkashi|flife__shot}}
  6. 1951

    The First Jnana Yajna

    In December, in a temple in Pune, he began the first of the lecture series he would call yajnas — Vedanta taught in English, in the open, to whoever came. Few came at first.

    {{FIG:yajna|flife__shot}}
  7. Forty years

    More Than Five Hundred Yajnas

    The teaching did not stop. Across four decades he conducted over five hundred lecture series, and around them grew study groups, publications, schools, hospitals and the residential Vedanta academies at Sandeepany.

  8. 1993

    Sidhbari

    He attained mahasamadhi in August 1993. His shrine is at Sidhbari, in Himachal Pradesh, under the mountains where he had studied.

The wider vision

A Vision Beyond
the Classroom.

His concern was not that India lacked schools. It was that a young person could pass through one, do well, and emerge with no working relationship to the civilisation they had been born into — competent, and unmoored.

What he argued for was an education that took character as seriously as it took examinations: cultural confidence rather than cultural nostalgia, discipline held from the inside, and the plain expectation that what a person knows is owed back to the people among whom they live.

The educational idea

Education as
Transformation.

Information can be handed over. A person cannot. The claim underneath every Chinmaya school is that education is only finished when the student has changed, and that this asks for the whole of them at once.

01

Body

Habit, stamina, sleep, sport and the ordinary discipline of looking after oneself — taught as the ground everything else stands on, not as a timetable slot.

02

Emotions

Steadiness under disappointment, and the capacity to want something for someone else. Boarding life teaches this whether a school intends it or not; the point is to intend it.

03

Intellect

Not the accumulation of answers but the habit of questioning — including questioning what the teacher has just said, which is how Vedanta has always been taught.

04

Spirit

The oldest question a school can put to a child, and the one most schools leave out: who is it that is learning all this, and what is it for.

From vision to institution

From a Vision
to a Campus.

The idea of an international school in India was his. Sites were weighed in Bangalore, Lucknow, the Andamans and Himachal Pradesh before Coimbatore was chosen, and the hundred acres in the Siruvani foothills were bought one rupee at a time.

In 1984 Swami Sahayanandaji walked the length and breadth of India, collecting a single rupee from each person towards the land. Ten years later Pujya Guruji Swami Tejomayananda took up the vision and carried it into being.

Gurudev did not see it open. He attained mahasamadhi in August 1993. On 6 June 1996 the Chinmaya International Residential School was inaugurated by Pujya Swami Chidanandaji, President of The Divine Life Society, with ninety-six students in Grades V to VIII and eleven academic staff.

The programme

The Chinmaya
Vision.

CIRS teaches through the Chinmaya Vision Programme, which integrates culture, philosophy and academics. It is not a subject on the timetable. It is the account of what the school is for, and it rests on four aspects.

Integrated
Development

The physical and mental conditions a young person actually needs in order to grow — addressed together, because they do not come apart.

Indian
Culture

Pride in the civilisation a student belongs to, taught closely enough to be understood rather than merely performed.

Patriotism

Team spirit and national feeling — the sense of owing something to a place larger than one’s own prospects.

Universal
Outlook

Respect and love for all living creatures, which is what keeps the previous aspect from curdling into something smaller.

At CIRS it shows up in the shape of the day rather than in a syllabus: morning assembly, prayer and aarti, yoga, value education, culture talks, service, the houses, the arts and sport, and the ordinary negotiations of living together on one campus.

The motto

ज्ञानं सेवा च कौशलम्

Jñānaṁ
Sevā ca
Kauśalam

Knowledge. Service. Efficiency.

कटिबद्धा वयं सर्वे लक्ष्यं साधयितुं वरम् |
शिक्षायास्त्रिविधं यद्वै ज्ञानं सेवा च कौशलम् ||

Knowledge is gained in order to serve the world efficiently. Learning that never reaches anyone is incomplete; service without skill is a good intention that does not arrive. The school asks for all three, and does not accept one of them as a substitute for the others.

The purpose

What Does
This Vision
Create?

The school states its aim plainly: to develop competent, sensitive and ethical young people, and to unlock the potential of exceptional children until they are conscientious global citizens.

Which is a description of a person, not of a result. Someone who can think hard and be argued with; who has the steadiness to be disappointed without being derailed; who knows where they come from without needing everyone else to come from the same place; and who, given something worth doing, is actually able to do it.

That is the whole of the claim. Everything else on this campus — two curricula, the houses, the field, the stage, the assembly — is the method.

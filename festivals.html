Through the year

The School Comes to Life.

CIRS is a bustling place, with vibrant festivals adorning its pathways through the year — and the school comes to life with melodious paeans and energetic dances for every one of them.

The enthusiasm resonates in each heart long after the event is over, because of the surge of participation from the fifth grade to the twelfth.

These festivities are not limited to the student body. The faculty are the backbone of every festival, lending unwavering support to every last-minute arrangement.

Celebrated at CIRS

Seven Festivals, One School.

In the order the school year meets them, from the monsoon to the spring. Every photograph here was taken at the festival it names, and is dated as closely as its camera allows.

01July–AugustA monsoon morning

Raksha Bandhan

Unfamiliar at first, students develop an unbreakable bond as brothers vow to protect their sisters. The programme begins with Krishnaji being tied the first rakhi. Siblings share their moments of love and mischief with the entire school during the morning pooja.

Then, while rakhis are being knotted, the school resonates with wishes and chocolate requests. As dusk falls, the brothers surprise their sisters with an emotional, relatable and heartfelt programme.

02August–SeptemberFrom morning into midnight

Janmashtami

The day begins with everyone seated in devotion for the pooja. A culmination of pure devotion and Kanha’s naughtiness radiates from every heart through the day, and students fast to express their bhakti towards the Supreme.

The midnight aarti and performance is packed with energy. Then comes the time to relive Krishna’s childhood, as each grade competes to be the first to break the matki.

03August–SeptemberFlowers, then the feast

Onam

Rooted in Kerala, Onam extends its roots all the way to CIRS, and is especially loved by every student — for its powerful symbol of sacrifice, and for the special feast, the sadhya.

After contenting hearts and stomachs, the spirited story of Mahabali, told through a skit, brings goosebumps to every spectator.

04August–SeptemberCloud on the hills

Ganesh Chaturthi

Mornings begin with the sacred worship of Vinayaka, and the joy of the prasad that follows. Evenings are filled with enthusiasm as every grade shows its devotion with renditions, dances and anecdotes for our dear Bappa.

At the core of the festival, the students handcraft their own murtis. As the season ends, everyone is on their toes, movements and voices in unison, as the procession carries the idol to be immersed in the school’s own lake.

05September–OctoberTorchlight after dusk

Dussehra

The day begins with the morning pooja, which brings a wave of calm. What truly brings the celebration to life are the mesmerising rangolis crafted by the students.

The school gathers to chant and witness the ultimate triumph of dharma — Ravan Dahan — and the evening sky is spellbound as the fireworks go up.

06JanuaryThe harvest sun

Pongal

Six hundred students, cooking in small groups.

One of the biggest harvest festivals, and the school is decorated in a majestic way. But the campus feels incomplete without the aroma of pongal swirling in the air, as six hundred students come together to prepare the delicacy in small groups.

Everyone goes around relishing the pongals prepared by the other teams — and then the winner of the most scrumptious pongal is declared.

Photographs of the cooking itself are still to be added.

07MarchColour on the field

Holi

The essence of the bonfire is brought to us through a skit in which Holika’s devious plans burn, and the countdown begins.

To CIRS, Holi brings a festive zeal of colours that soon find their owners. The Dronacharya field buzzes with excitement as colour paints every face.

Beyond the school calendar

Festivals across India

A wider calendar of traditions. Inclusion here does not imply a documented CIRS event.

Most of these follow lunar or regional calendars, so their dates move from year to year; the months given are the usual range.

January–February March–May June–September October–December Through the year

January–February

  • Lohri

    Around 13 January

    A winter bonfire festival of Punjab and much of north India, with songs, dancing and offerings of peanuts, rewri and popcorn to the fire.

  • Makar Sankranti

    Mid-January

    The sun's passage into Makara, kept across India under many names — kites for Uttarayan in Gujarat, sesame and jaggery in Maharashtra, new-rice sweets in Bengal.

  • Basant Panchami

    January–February

    The fifth day of Magha, welcoming spring. In many homes and schools it is Saraswati's day, the goddess of learning, and a day for yellow.

  • Maha Shivaratri

    February–March

    A night of fasting and vigil for Shiva, kept in temples and homes across the country.

March–May

  • Ugadi and Gudi Padwa

    March–April

    The lunisolar New Year: Ugadi in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, with its chutney of six tastes; Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra and Goa, with a gudi raised at the door.

  • Ram Navami

    March–April

    The birth of Rama, on the ninth day of Chaitra, marked with readings of the Ramayana, temple worship and processions.

  • Mahavir Jayanti

    March–April

    The birth of Vardhamana Mahavira, the twenty-fourth Tirthankara of the Jain tradition, with processions, worship and giving.

  • Easter

    March–April

    The Christian feast of the Resurrection, closing Holy Week, kept by churches across India.

  • Baisakhi

    13 or 14 April

    The spring harvest festival of Punjab, when the wheat is ready. For Sikhs it also remembers the founding of the Khalsa in 1699.

  • The New Year in mid-April

    Around 14 April

    The solar New Year, under many names: Tamil Puthandu, Vishu in Kerala, Bohag Bihu in Assam, Pohela Boishakh in Bengal and Maha Vishuva Sankranti in Odisha.

    At CIRSVishu and Tamil Puthandu were kept in the CIRS dorms in April 2025 — see News.

  • Buddha Purnima

    April–May

    The full moon remembering the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and passing, also called Buddha Jayanti.

June–September

  • Rath Yatra

    June–July

    Jagannath, Balabhadra and Subhadra drawn through the streets of Puri on great wooden chariots in the month of Ashadha, with processions in many other cities.

October–December

  • Navaratri and Durga Puja

    September–October

    Nine nights of the Goddess: garba and dandiya in Gujarat, golu in the south, and in Bengal Durga Puja, recognised by UNESCO in 2021 as intangible cultural heritage.

    At CIRSThe school's photo archive holds Navaratri evenings at CIRS from 2023 to 2025; their story is still to be written here.

  • Diwali

    October–November

    The festival of lamps. Hindus mark Rama's return and worship Lakshmi, Jains the nirvana of Mahavira, and Sikhs keep Bandi Chhor Divas on the same day.

  • Chhath

    October–November

    Four days of worship of the Sun in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, with offerings at the riverbank to both the setting and the rising sun.

  • Guru Nanak Gurpurab

    October–November

    The birth of Guru Nanak, founder of the Sikh faith, on the full moon of Kartik: a continuous reading of the Guru Granth Sahib, processions and langar.

  • Christmas

    25 December

    The birth of Jesus, kept by Christian communities across India with midnight services, carols and paper stars.

Through the year

  • Eid al-Fitr

    Moves about eleven days earlier each year

    The end of Ramadan, the month of fasting, with morning prayers, new clothes and meals shared with family and neighbours.

  • Eid al-Adha

    Moves about eleven days earlier each year

    The Feast of Sacrifice at the close of the Hajj, remembering Ibrahim's devotion, with prayers and meat shared with family, neighbours and those in need.

Summarised from Encyclopaedia Britannica, Incredible India (Ministry of Tourism) and the UNESCO intangible heritage list.

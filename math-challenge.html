Visual Geometry Challenge

The shortest path: Heron’s reflection.

Before exploring the school’s monthly archive, try this classic geometric puzzle. Move the point along the baseline to discover the principle light itself obeys.

Sample Interactive Puzzle

Heron of Alexandria’s Shortest Path

Editorial demonstration • Not an official monthly paper

Instruction: Two points, A and B, sit above a reflective line L. Move point P along line L so that the total walking journey AP + PB is as short as mathematically possible.

Segment AP --
Segment PB --
Total Length (AP + PB) --
Incident Angle α --°
Reflection Angle β --°

Tip: Use Left & Right arrow keys on your keyboard, drag Point P directly on screen, or slide above.

Geometric Hint

  1. What happens if you reflect point B straight down across line L to create a virtual mirror point B′?
  2. For any position of P, length PB is identical to length PB′ by symmetry. Therefore, minimizing AP + PB is the exact same as minimizing AP + PB′!
  3. What is the shortest geometric path connecting point A to point B′?
Mathematical Insight

Why the Shortest Path Creates Equal Angles

In Euclidean geometry, the shortest distance between any two points is a straight line segment. By reflecting point B across line L to virtual point B′, we notice that PB = PB′ for any point P on line L.

Therefore, the total path length AP + PB = AP + PB′. The path is minimized when A, P, and B′ lie on one straight, unbroken line!

Because vertical opposite angles are equal, this collinear line guarantees that the incident angle α equals the reflection angle β. Heron of Alexandria discovered this geometric minimum in 60 CE — this is the exact principle of least time by which light reflects from mirrors.

The Challenge Journey

How a mind solves: Five stages, one problem.

Every challenge begins with a question. Here is how mathematical habits of mind — questioning, finding invariants, testing small cases, and rigorous deduction — turn an impossible brute-force search into an instant proof.

Stage 1: The Question
  1. 01 Question

    The Mutilated Chessboard

    Technique: Read it twice. Decide what is actually being asked, and what is only decoration.

    An 8×8 board has 64 squares. Cut away two diagonally opposite corners, leaving 62 squares. Can you tile the remaining surface with 31 dominoes of size 2×1 without overlap?

    Question technique: strip decorative constraints; express the board strictly by its boundary geometry.

  2. 02 Think

    Search for an Invariant

    Technique: Look for the pattern, the symmetry, the thing that stays the same while everything else moves.

    Observe what does not change: regardless of where a 2×1 domino is laid horizontally or vertically, it must cover two adjacent squares that share an edge.

    Thinking technique: do not start testing arrangements; search for a conserved property (an invariant).

  3. 03 Explore

    The Minimal 2×2 Case

    Technique: Try the small case. Draw it. Guess, then test the guess and find out why it failed.

    Reduce to a 2×2 grid with opposite corners excised: only 2 squares remain. Can one domino cover them? No — they only touch diagonally at a vertex, sharing no edge.

    Exploration technique: minimal cases expose geometric edge-sharing rules that brute force obscures.

  4. 04 Solve

    Color Parity Deduction

    Technique: Build the argument step by step, and check that each step follows from the one before it.

    Color the 64 squares like a chessboard: 32 dark, 32 light. Diagonally opposite corners always share the same color! Removing two dark corners leaves 30 dark and 32 light squares. But every 2×1 domino covers exactly 1 dark and 1 light square. Thirty-one dominoes require 31 dark and 31 light squares. Since 30 ≠ 32, complete tiling is mathematically impossible!

    Solving technique: coloring transforms an impossible brute-force search into a deterministic parity proof.

  5. 05 Discover

    The Power of Invariants

    Technique: Ask what the problem was really about — that is the part that carries to the next one.

    Without parity, checking all possible placements would require over 2×1015 tests. By identifying an invariant, the problem resolves in seconds. This principle carries forward to graph theory, topology, and algorithm design.

    Discovery technique: the best solution changes how you view structure, not merely the answer to one question.
